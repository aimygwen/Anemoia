Log · Vibe shit

8 Little Bit of Nostalgia

Holo-stickers, acrylic glare, and the clicky click snap of arcade sounds.

1 Poly... 2 Poly...

My style is a love letter to that era. In a world where modern visuals constantly drift into generic, overly sterile corporate looks, I found myself running full speed in the opposite direction.

I wanted to bring back the fun—that heartwarming, playful energy where every pixel felt like it had a soul. By pairing clean, minimal visual layouts with a retro vibe I just can't let go of, I'm building a space where nostalgic hardware aesthetics meet crisp, modern design.

Glitter, Gloss and a touch of Chaos.

This is about physical texture breaking through digital screens.

It's the tactile satisfaction of 90s holo-cards, clear plastic game cartridges, acrylic keychains, and glossy stickers. Designing digitally, but thinking physically—making every asset feel like something you want to physically hold, trade, or collect.

Oh look, an ultra rare!!

Styled like a custom holo-sticker with bubbly Powerpuff Girls-esque proportions, the “Aimy Gwen” Wordmark goes full Y2K to inject pure, nostalgic playfulness into my world.

It acts as the sticker slapped onto the base—tying the overall visual identity straight back to physical merch culture.

Charmingly Flowless

My visual identity needed to feel like a physical artifact—something you could hold, collect, or slap onto a retro console.

Finding its shape meant wading through a mess of early sketches, balancing crisp minimalism with genuine warmth. After endless iteration, it clicked: a high-shine mascot inspired by custom acrylic keychains, laced with subtle holo-glitter. Clean, minimal, and full of character. I was first inspired by Apple's liquid glass. It sparked some nostalgia in me, until I realized why. It felt like something nostalgic, but new. Like a certain aquatic design material that felt like that if you saw it you wanted to lick it. If there's a true liquid glass material in the real world, I think I know where to find it.

High Voltage

I love being surrounded by beautiful things. Vibrant neon colors and soft pastel palettes cutting through beautiful city architecture while vibing music plays all stacking up, all at once. It's a beautiful, energizing rush that you bring all the way home and pour into your work.

Then the layer count gets too high.

I mean, it's still beautiful. Just… sometimes I wish it could all shut up for a moment. It's not like I don't like all the overwhelming, beautiful noise rippling down on me, but—well, we all need that stimulation of things happening around us, right?

It's just that the colors start humming too loud. What felt like creative fuel slowly turns into a crowded room with no windows, pressing against your chest until everything feels like a lot. Too much and your eyes just get tired, the hands freeze.

And you reach a point where you just want it all to stop. Where your ears just scream for—

Silence.

Choosing Companions

My Everyday Helping Hands

At the very center of everything sits my iPad Pro, my main companion for drawing, where the distance between a sudden thought and the first stroke on canvas is as close to zero as it gets.

Instead of suffocating under heavily expensive legacy creative suites (pointing at you, Adobe.), I've built an agile stack of tools I actually enjoy opening every day. My primary apps to go are the Affinity Suite for vector precision and image work, paired with Blockbench when it's time to shape low-poly meshes and voxel structures.

When ideas demand quick brain-to-canvas ideation, I jump into Procreate. For animated pieces, Procreate Dreams is a dream (hah.) for frame-by-frame illustration, cutting the final sequences natively in Final Cut Pro.

I'm slowly leveling up my secondary stats in Blender for lighting and spatial builds, while actively phasing out Adobe tools in favor of independent apps that stay fast, lightweight, and out of my way.

And out of my wallet.

How ?

I'm a shaper, not a carver. To carve, you have to know the exact shape hiding inside the block before you even lay a finger on your tools. But shaping is organic — it takes form as you touch it, shifting through trial, error, and dynamic momentum.

Log · Hytale

Models & UV structure

How Hytale assets get blocked, unwrapped, and packed so they stay readable in-game — not just in a render.

Blockout first

Silhouette before polish. I keep face counts honest early so proportion reads at gameplay distance — if the shape only works in a turntable, it does not ship. Extension sets share a visual dialect: rounded corners, readable material breaks, no noise for noise's sake.

UV mapping

UVs are layout design. Islands get grouped by material story, not by what was convenient in edit mode. Texel density is matched across a set so a furnishing does not glow sharper than its neighbor. Seams hide in creases and undersides — places the camera and the player both forgive.

Structure & variants

Variant swaps live on predictable islands so recolors do not reshuffle the whole sheet. Naming follows the pack, not my mood. When a mesh needs nine colorways, the UV plan is decided before the first texture pass — otherwise every variant becomes a small emergency.
